How it works

LifeMeter turns population data into a picture of your time. It is an estimate, not a prediction.

1. A baseline from where you live

LifeMeter starts from life expectancy at birth for your country and sex, using the World Bank’s World Development Indicators (indicators SP.DYN.LE00.FE.IN and SP.DYN.LE00.MA.IN). The World Bank takes these figures from the UN Population Division’s World Population Prospects. The app uses the most recent year available (currently 2024) and covers 217 countries and territories. If you choose “Somewhere else”, it uses the world average. If you’d rather not say your sex, it uses the average of the two.

2. Optional nudges for five habits

After the reveal you can answer five optional questions. Each answer shifts the estimate by a few years at most, and the total is capped to a plausible range.

HabitEvidence
SmokingJha et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2013
Physical activityMoore et al., PLOS Medicine, 2012
AlcoholWood et al., The Lancet, 2018
Body weightProspective Studies Collaboration, The Lancet, 2009
DietFadnes et al., PLOS Medicine, 2022

3. A range, never a date

LifeMeter shows the end of the estimate as a range, such as “around 2060–2067”, and never as a single date.

4. The Life Clock

The share of the estimate you’ve lived is mapped onto a 24-hour day. At 25% it’s 6:00 AM; at 50% it’s noon. The same number drives the weeks, days, hours and seconds left, the life-in-weeks grid and the “tail end” counts (summers, weekends, full moons, books and sunrises).

What this is not. Life expectancy at birth is an average for people born now, under today’s conditions. Your own life depends on your genes, health care, circumstances and luck. It also understates how long older people typically live, because they have already survived earlier risks. Treat the number as a prompt, not a verdict, and not as medical advice.
